MY FOCUSRequirements · Interaction direction · Handoff

Equipment records, site certificates, inspections and smoke alerts belong to one operational system. They only become useful when each person knows what to do next.

The challenge

A site owner, field inspector, installation team and administrator see different parts of the same work. An unclear entry point, mismatched task status or ambiguous completion step can interrupt the handoff between them.

The design problem extends beyond individual screens: it includes the business object being managed, the person responsible and the evidence of the next action.

My contribution

I prepared overseas requirements discussions, clarified site and role boundaries, reviewed cross-app interactions and helped turn observed issues into prioritised fixes and checks.

I worked with product, backend and hardware colleagues. Engineering teammates and AI-assisted workflows supported implementation while I focused on requirements, interaction review and delivery coordination.

02 / REQUIREMENTS

Start with the right
unit of work.

Stakeholder clarification shaped the site model and the division of responsibilities.

What needed clarification

Before an overseas discussion, I identified questions about what a certificate covered, how related charges were separated, who installed smoke detectors and which interface should hold those responsibilities.

I requested an English discussion guide, then supplied the meeting transcript for follow-up synthesis. The stakeholder input helped distinguish site certification from the connected-device service.

What I took forward

I proposed allowing an owner to manage multiple sites. Later, I clarified that a site's personal or organisational classification should not be duplicated as an account type during registration.

This kept two concepts separate: who signs in and which places they manage. Installation responsibilities also needed their own operational surface rather than being placed indiscriminately in the owner or inspector app.

Put each choice where it belongs.

Account registration should establish the person. Site management should establish the premises. Installation and inspection should follow their actual responsibilities.

03 / INTERACTION DIRECTION

Keep the task moving.

Reviewing hierarchy, status and transitions across the mobile apps and admin console.

I tested and reviewed the interfaces as connected tasks, asking what an operator should understand, choose and see after each action.

01

Show the problem, not just a category.

I asked for specific status wording rather than a generic warning or non-compliance label, supported by a consistent severity colour. I also reviewed drawer details and redundant close controls in the admin interface.

Accept a task, then continue doing it.

On the inspector app, I wanted the assignment itself to have visual priority. After acceptance, the flow should continue into inspection instead of bouncing the person back to the list and forcing another search.

Use a shared interaction language.

I requested alignment between the installation app and the existing owner/inspector apps, including navigation, refresh behaviour, input and feedback. Earlier testing identified missing back navigation, unclear completion feedback and card layouts with weak information hierarchy.

04 / WORKFLOW REFINEMENT

An alert needs
a clear next action.

Moving from signal presentation to an understandable dispatch workspace.

Revisit who actually responds

I raised the need for a site owner to report a fire and clarified that the inspector app was used for inspection staff, not every emergency responder. This moved emergency coordination towards the admin workspace.

I discussed location confirmation, accidental activation, responsible operators and contact information, while reviewing AI-assisted proposals for the case workflow and shared editing.

Let testing change the scope

After trying the flow, I found acceptance and completion unclear. I questioned the long reporting sequence because we had not yet established the real dispatcher's reporting needs.

I proposed a smaller first workflow centred on confirming the responding unit. I then asked how a dispatched alert would leave the map, exposing the difference between sending help and confirming that an incident had ended.

The resulting direction

The subsequent team specification separated the dispatch queue from confirmation that an incident had ended. It retained site contacts, visible progress and accountable actions while reducing routine form filling. Device state and administrative case status remained distinct.

My role in the decision. I challenged confusing transitions, narrowed the first scope and reviewed operational boundaries. AI and engineering contributed detailed case-state, editing and implementation solutions.

What was delivered

The project record includes signed Android build handoffs, role-specific usage guides, test instructions and later workflow revisions. Together, these artifacts connect product decisions with implementation and operator handoff.

Before a planned demonstration, I requested a return to a known-working build while keeping newer changes available for later iteration. This kept demonstration readiness separate from subsequent product iteration.

Keep each stage visible

Release records distinguish active builds, inactive candidates and demonstration packages. Hardware checks and human acceptance were tracked separately from automated or simulated verification.

This distinction guided the next step: a build can be ready for a scoped check while the wider service continues through hardware testing, operator feedback and acceptance.
